Start hereHow to use this guide

This guide does one job: it takes you from a blank page to your first finished deck, one clear step at a time, and it tells you what to do when something goes wrong.

What this guide is (and is not)

  • It is a workbook. Every part ends with a short checklist. Tick the boxes as you go. Your ticks and your notes are saved in this browser, and they print too.
  • It follows the real app. Every step uses the buttons and words you see in Bluff Cabaret AI: Setup, Paste & Check, Production Script, Print Kit.
  • It is honest. The app is a prompt engine. It never draws cards itself. You paste its prompts into your own ChatGPT, which creates the artwork. That takes real work, and this guide shows you how to do it in the smartest order.
  • It makes no income promises. What you earn, if anything, depends on your topic, your listing, your marketing and your effort. This guide gives you a faster, clearer, repeatable way to create and test a real product. That is all it claims.

The one rule that saves the most time

Do the cheap tests first. A topic costs nothing to change. A text card costs a few minutes. An illustrated card costs real effort. A printed deck costs money. So: score the topic, verify the facts, playtest the text, and only then spend your time on artwork. This order runs through the whole guide.

Bluff Cabaret AI

The planner

Writes prompts. Checks your deck for repeated cards, weak bluffs, risky facts and balance. Orders the production. Builds the print kit, the rulebook and the verification file.

It never draws.

ChatGPT

The writer and the illustrator

It can make mistakes.

You

The editor and the publisher

Choose the topic. Verify the facts. Approve every image. Print and test. Playtest. Write an honest listing. Decide what to do next.

You are responsible for the final product.

Why the order is fixed

The style sheet comes first so every later image can match it. The cast comes before the cards because the characters appear as witnesses on the cards. The cards come last because they depend on both. If you change the order, the deck drifts. If you follow it, the deck looks like it came from one studio.

The three games inside one box

Every deck contains three ways to bluff. Knowing them helps you judge your topic, because each mode needs a different kind of fact.

ModeHow it playsWhat it needs from the topic
Real or BluffA cast member gives a witness line. A claim is read aloud. Everyone secretly plays a TRUST or BLUFF chip. The reveal shows the truth.Surprising true facts, and plausible false ones that sound just as good.
Spot the FakeFour statements, A to D. Three are real and one is false. Everyone secretly plays the letter they think is fake.Groups of related facts where a false one can hide naturally.

The full game has 10 Real or Bluff cards, 8 Spot the Fake cards and 6 Invent the Bluff cards, in three galleries of eight. The short game has 5, 4 and 3, in three galleries of four.

How to pick the world

  1. Start with the buyer, not with yourself. Who gets the game? Kids and families? Adults who like mysteries? Teachers? The world's age fit is shown in the app. Noir Hoax Bureau is aimed at teens and adults. The others are family-friendly.
  2. Look at the topic ideas you already have. Which world do they fit naturally? A heist topic in Atlas feels strange. The same topic in Menagerie Cabaret feels built for it.
  3. Imagine the thumbnail. Look at the palette and the cast. Which one would stop a scroll in a crowded search page?
  4. Prefer the world you enjoy looking at. You will look at its images dozens of times while you build. Pick one you do not tire of.

Step 2: choose a topic

The topic is the subject of the whole deck: "Heists and stolen masterpieces", "Islands on maps that were never there", "The animals who flew before astronauts". Every one of the 24 (or 12) cards is a true story from inside that subject, plus a convincing bluff.

The eight questions every topic must pass

  1. Is it true, and documented? Can you find at least two independent, reliable sources for the key facts? If the facts live only in rumours or one blog, walk away.
  2. Is it surprising? The game works because some true things sound false. A topic full of obvious facts produces a boring game.
  3. Is it deep enough? A 12-card deck needs about a dozen strong stories. A 24-card deck needs more. If you can think of only five, the topic is too narrow.
  4. Is it varied enough for three galleries? The cards are split into three galleries named by the world. Can your topic break naturally into three sub-areas?
  5. Is it easy to bluff? A good bluff sounds as plausible as the truth. Topics with numbers, dates, records and "firsts" bluff well. Topics where the truth is vague do not.
  6. Is it safe for the audience? Avoid graphic violence, politics, religion, medical or legal claims and brand names. The app's Fact & Bluff Check flags these words, so a topic that depends on them will fight you.
  7. Does it look good? Can you picture ten different illustrated scenes? A topic with strong objects, places and creatures illustrates well. A topic made of abstract ideas does not.
  8. Would a stranger understand it from the title? "Famous heists" is clear in two seconds. "The socio-economics of early banking" is not.
Walk away from these topics
  • Anything where the "facts" are contested, political or religious.
  • Anything about real, living, private people.
  • Topics built around brands, logos or franchises you do not own.
  • Health, safety or legal claims presented as advice.
  • Anything you cannot verify with two sources.

The topic scoring sheet

Fill in up to three candidate topics. Give each question a score from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent). The sheet adds the totals for you. Quick name check

Your deck title and any series name should not clash with an existing product. This is not legal advice, only a sensible first filter. Do it before you spend hours on artwork.

  1. Search the exact title in a web search engine. Look at the first two pages.
  2. Search it inside the marketplace where you plan to sell.
  3. Search it together with "card game" and "printable".
  4. If anything looks the same or confusingly close, change your title. Keep a note of what you checked and the date.
  5. If you plan to build a brand around a series name, check trademark databases for your country or ask a professional.
Give every deck its own title

Use a descriptive title for each deck, plus a series or shop name of your own if you like. Before you use the name of the system itself in a listing, read the license terms that came with your purchase.

Six reasons to start short

  1. You finish. A finished small deck teaches you more than an unfinished large one. The motivation of holding a real, complete product is worth a lot.
  2. You find your mistakes cheaply. Style problems, text problems and printing problems all appear in the first deck. Finding them at 39 prompts costs far less than at 63.
  3. Verification is manageable. Twelve cards means twelve sets of facts to check. That is an afternoon, not a week.
  4. It is a complete, playable game. The short game uses the same three modes and the same cast. It is not a demo. It is a smaller edition.
  5. It is a natural product tier. A starter deck and a complete deck are two different listings at two different price points. You can make both.
  6. It leaves room to grow. You can add the 12-card Expansion to the same world later, or build a full game on the same topic once the short one has proved itself.

Audience, tone and difficulty

  • Audience changes the wording and the safety of the cards. Pick the group you actually imagine buying the deck. Do not pick "everyone".
  • Tone changes the witness lines and the voice of the reveals. Playful works for families. Curious and Smart suits adults.
  • Difficulty curve decides how the point values are spread. Mixed is the safest first choice. Easy suits younger players and classrooms.

"Used So Far": how multi-part prompts work

A long deck cannot always be written in one answer. The app splits the prompt into parts. The routine is always the same:

  1. Paste Part 1In a new ChatGPT chat. Wait for the full answer.
  2. Paste the reply into "Used So Far"This tells the app what has already been written, so Part 2 can list everything used and never repeat a card.
  3. Copy Part 2 into the same chatKeep the same conversation so ChatGPT remembers the context. Repeat until the last part.
Do not mix chats

If you start a new chat in the middle, ChatGPT forgets the cards already written and may repeat them. If you must start over, paste the finished cards into "Used So Far" first.

The Game Balance check: what it looks for

It checks the fixed blueprint of the deck: the number of cards in each mode, the letters of the answers, the witnesses, the galleries, the difficulty, the Witness Lines, the Tells and the points. When something is off, it tells you in plain words what to change, for example "Change 2 of the 8 cards to BLUFF by rewriting the statement".

Why balance matters

If too many answers are REAL, or the right letter is almost always C, players learn the pattern and stop thinking about the facts. A balanced deck stays fair on the second and third play, and that is what earns recommendations.

Part 5Verify the facts before you spend a minute on art

A fact game lives or dies on accuracy. One wrong "fact" in a deck you are selling is a bad review waiting to happen. This is a routine, not a talent: anyone can do it, and it takes only an afternoon for a short deck.

Why this part comes before the artwork

If you find a wrong fact after the images are made, you must regenerate and re-approve the card. If you find it now, you only edit a line of text. Verify first. It is the cheapest correction you will ever make.

What the app gives you

  • DATA_TO_VERIFY. Press Download DATA_TO_VERIFY in Step 4. It is a file that lists every card the checks marked as worth a second look, including the claims that need a source.
  • Source links from the topic bank. When a topic comes from Surprise Me, the topic card lists source links. Open them as your starting point.
  • Widely documented facts only. The prompts ask ChatGPT for facts that are widely documented. This reduces errors but does not remove them. AI can still make mistakes.

The two-source rule

A fact counts as verified only when two independent, reliable sources agree with it, and you have written down where. "I looked it up and it seemed right" does not count.

Good sourcesUse with careDo not rely on
Museums, archives and libraries; government and national bodies; universities; established encyclopedias; reputable news organisations reporting at the time; books by recognised experts.Wikipedia (excellent as a map to its own cited sources, so follow the references); company websites for facts about the company; popular-science articles.Anonymous blogs, social media posts, quote collections, AI answers, "did you know" lists without sources.

The verification routine

  1. Download DATA_TO_VERIFY and open your deckWork in the order of the card numbers. Keep the log (Appendix B) next to you.
  2. Take one cardRead the Reveal. Underline every checkable claim: names, dates, places, numbers, "first", "only", "largest".
  3. Find source 1Search the claim itself, not the topic. Open a reliable page. Copy the exact fact and the link into the log.
  4. Find source 2Use a different organisation, not a copy of the first. If both pages quote the same original, count them as one.
  5. Compare the detailsDo dates and numbers match exactly? If sources disagree, you have found a card to soften, rewrite or replace.
  6. DecideMark the card Verified, Rewritten (new wording that both sources support) or Replaced (a new story).
  7. Verify the bluff tooCheck that the false statement is genuinely false and could not be read as true. A bluff that happens to be true ruins the card.
  8. Update the deckMake the change in ChatGPT with the app's Fix prompt, or edit the card text directly, and run the checks again.

A worked example

Take the claim "The Mona Lisa was stolen from the Louvre in 1911 and returned to the museum a couple of years later."

Claim partSource 1Source 2Result
Stolen from the LouvreMuseum's own history pageA major encyclopedia entryAgree. Verified.
Year 1911Encyclopedia entryA reputable news archiveAgree. Verified.
"Returned two years later"Encyclopedia entry (recovered end of 1913)Museum history (returned to the Louvre in January 1914)Recovery and return are different dates, so the wording "about two years" is fine. Do not say "returned in 1913".

This shows the most common trap: two real dates that sound the same. Writing the claim at the precision the sources support keeps the card true.

Soften instead of deleting

If a number is hard to confirm, rewrite the card around the part you can prove. "More than twenty" is safer than "exactly 21" when only one source gives the exact figure.

Time-box it

Give each card a fixed slot, for example ten minutes. If you cannot verify a claim in that time, rewrite the card or replace it. Do not let one stubborn claim eat your day.

Sensitive claims

  • Never state anything about living private people.
  • Avoid medical, legal and safety advice, even as a joke.
  • Treat anything about disasters or violence with respect and keep it brief. The target audience decides how much detail is acceptable.
  • Do not use a brand, logo or character you do not own.

Keep the paper trail

Keep your fact log with the project file. If a buyer ever questions a card, you can answer quickly with the sources. It also makes your next deck faster, because many sources overlap inside a topic area.

Part 6Get a deck that looks like one product

AI art drifts. Ask for image after image and the style changes, characters change faces and text slides off the card. This part is the method that keeps 39 or 63 images looking like they came from one studio.

The five principles

1. One chat or project

Keep the whole production in one ChatGPT chat or project, so the style you approved stays in view.

2. Style first

Approve the Style Reference Sheet before anything else. Everything after must match it.

3. Approved images as reference

Every new image prompt is sent with the approved images attached. Reference beats description.

4. Follow the order

Style, cast, fixed components, cards by gallery, promo. The Production Script is already in this order.

5. Approve, do not accept

Check every image against a list before you keep it. Regenerate one card, not the deck.

Before you generate anything

  1. Open Step 3 and compile the Production ScriptIt is ordered: style sheet first, then cast, then cards, gallery by gallery.
  2. Choose the text handlingIn Card Production Options you can choose "Full finished card with text (recommended)" or "Art only, I will add text in Canva". Full text is faster. Art only gives you total control of lettering but needs a layout tool and more of your time. For a first deck, use the recommended option.
  3. Create the folder structureSee the naming system below.
  4. Start a new ChatGPT chat or projectName it with your deck title and the date.
  5. Paste the Style Bible onceUse Copy Style Bible. It is the text that describes the look: art style, frame, palette, layout and trim of your world. Paste it at the start of the chat, then generate the Style Reference Sheet.

Approving the Style Reference Sheet

This single image decides how the whole deck will look. Take your time. Approve it only if every box is ticked.

Keeping the cast consistent

  • Generate the Cast Sheet (all 5) first, then each cast card one by one. Approve each character. Save them.
  • For every later card where a character appears as a witness, attach that character's approved cast card as a reference. Do not rely on the name alone.
  • If a character's face changes in a card, regenerate that card with the cast card attached. Do not "fix" it by describing the face in words.

The reference rule

Always attach

For each new image attach: (1) the approved Style Reference Sheet, (2) the approved cast card of any character who appears, (3) one or two already-approved cards from the same gallery. Then paste the prompt. If the tool lets you hold only a few images at once, prefer the style sheet and the nearest approved card.

Output size and the safe area

Each prompt starts with an exact output-size instruction. Leave it as it is. The cards are composed for a 63 x 88 mm card (5:7) inside a taller canvas, with every important element, such as text panels, seals and characters, kept in the central area and the background extended to the edges as bleed. This is what makes the card survive cutting.

Part 7Fix a single bad card instead of redoing the deck

Some cards will come back wrong. That is normal. The professional response is to fix that one card. This part gives you a decision tree and the exact method.

Diagnose first

What is wrong?Where the problem livesWhat to do
The text on the image is wrong or misspelledThe image.Use the card's Regenerate with exact text prompt. Same style and composition, the strings exactly as given.
The image is fine but the fact is wrongThe deck text.Fix the card text in the app first (Part 5), then regenerate the images of that card.
The composition or a character is wrongThe image.Regenerate with the style sheet and the cast card attached.
The card is boring or confusing in playThe card idea.Replace the card. Ask for a new card with the app's Fix prompt, then produce its images.
The Question and Reveal faces do not matchOne of the two images.Regenerate the weaker face with the other attached as reference.

The "Regenerate with exact text" routine

  1. Find the card in the Production ScriptEach card has two entries, for example "Card 7 (Question)" and "Card 7 (Reveal)". Each has its own regenerate prompt and a verification checklist.
  2. Open the same ChatGPT chatStay in the production chat so the style stays in view. If you must use a new chat, attach the style sheet and a sibling card first.
  3. Attach the referencesThe style sheet, the cast card of the witness and one approved card from the same gallery.
  4. Paste the regenerate promptIt tells the generator to keep the same style and composition and to write the strings exactly as given.
  5. Check against the card's checklistEvery string, every number, every letter.
  6. Save as v2Keep v1 for a week in case v2 is worse.
Limit your retries

If three attempts fail, change something: shorten the text, simplify the scene, or attach a different reference. Repeating the same request tends to give the same error.

When to replace a card entirely

  • You cannot verify the fact with two sources.
  • Playtesters never understood it, or always guessed it right.
  • It repeats another card's idea.
  • It depends on a sensitive claim.
  • It has failed three regenerations.

Use the Fix prompt in the app to get a replacement that fits the blueprint (same mode, same gallery, same points), then verify it like any other card, and only then produce its images.

Part 8The home-printing test before you commit

Printing has traps: sizes, bleed, crop marks and double-sided alignment. One mistake wastes a sheet, or a whole order. A single test costs a few sheets and removes the risk.

What the Print Kit gives you

  • Card trim 63 x 88 mm (5:7, Poker size), composed inside a taller canvas, with the background extended past the trim as bleed.
  • Print sheets 3 x 3 cards per sheet on A4 or US Letter with crop marks. You upload your approved card images to ChatGPT and paste one sheet prompt at a time. The number of sheets for your deck is listed in the app.
  • Mirrored reveal sheets so the Reveal faces line up behind the Question faces when you print double-sided and flip on the long edge.
  • The Host Guide a one-page how-to-play. Its text is built from the fixed rulebook, so ChatGPT never improvises a rule.
  • The fixed rulebook the exact rules for your settings, ready to print.

Equipment and materials

ItemRecommendationWhy
Paper280 to 350 gsm matte card or photo paper.Heavier stock shuffles better and hides the reveal on the back. Check what your printer can feed.
Test paperOrdinary plain paper.For the first alignment test. No need to waste card stock.
CuttingA paper trimmer or a craft knife with a metal ruler and a cutting mat.Clean straight cuts matter more than anything else for a good-looking deck.
Corner rounder (optional)A small corner punch.Makes the cards feel like real playing cards.
Sleeves (optional)Standard Poker size sleeves.Protects and hides small cutting differences. If you sleeve, you may not need to glue.
Glue stick (optional)If you print fronts and backs separately.Alternative to double-sided printing.

The printing test, step by step

  1. Set the printer to 100%Choose "Actual size" or 100%. Never "Fit to page" or "Scale to fit". Scaling breaks the 63 x 88 mm size and the alignment.
  2. Print one question sheet on plain paperSingle-sided. Measure a card with a ruler: it should be 63 mm wide and 88 mm tall.
  3. Print the matching reveal sheet on the backPrint double-sided and flip on the long edge. Hold the page up to the light and check that front and back frames line up.
  4. Note the offsetIf the back is shifted, write down by how much and in which direction. Many printers have a small, consistent offset. Some drivers let you correct it.
  5. Print one proof on card stockUse the real paper. Check colour, contrast and how the ink dries.
  6. Cut the proofCut along the crop marks. Check that no white edge shows and that no text is cut.
  7. Shuffle and handle itDo the cards feel right? Is the back visible through the card? Do edges curl?

Troubleshooting

ProblemLikely causeFix
Cards are smaller than 63 x 88 mmThe printer scaled the page.Turn off Fit to page. Set 100%.
Back is shifted compared with the frontWrong flip direction, or printer offset.Use long-edge flip. If it is still off, nudge in the print dialog or adjust the page position.
Back is upside downShort-edge flip chosen.Reprint with long-edge flip.
White edge after cuttingCut drifted, or bleed missing.Cut slightly outside the crop mark inside the bleed. Use a trimmer with a guide.
Colours look dull or differentPaper and printer profile.Try photo paper. Test with "high quality". Accept that screens and printers differ. Do not promise exact colours in your listing.
Ink smears or the sheet curlsPaper too thin or too much ink.Use a heavier matte paper. Let the sheet dry before printing the back.
Printer cannot feed card stockThe tray limit.Use the rear feed, thinner stock or a print shop.
Do not promise print quality

Results depend on the buyer's printer and paper. In your listing, say "test print first" and describe the recommended paper. Do not promise a particular finish.

If you use a print service

  • Ask the service for their own template and bleed requirements. Do not assume they match yours.
  • Order a single proof before a batch.
  • Ask whether they print double-sided and how they handle the flip.
  • Check the proof for cut position, colour and the feel of the stock.

Part 9Playtest with three friends

You cannot see your own game clearly. Fresh players can. A single honest playtest finds more problems than a week of solo checking.

When to test: two rounds

RoundWhat you testWhy then
Text playtest (Day 4)The card text printed on plain paper, no art.It tests the facts, the bluffs and the rules before any artwork exists. Problems cost minutes to fix.
Final playtest (Day 7)The printed, cut, finished deck.It tests readability, the look and the feel of the real product.

Prepare

  • Players: three friends, plus you as facilitator (you observe, you do not play). Three players is a valid game. You may use up to five.
  • Mix: include one person who is not a gamer, and one who is. If your audience is kids or a classroom, test with someone from that group.
  • Materials: the cards, the rulebook, TRUST and BLUFF chips and A, B, C, D chips for each player (or paper markers), pen and your playtest sheet.
  • Time: block enough time for a full game plus a short chat afterwards.

The script

  1. Welcome (1 minute)"Thank you for helping. I am testing the game, not you. If something confuses you, that is useful information. Please say what you think out loud."
  2. Do not teach more than the rulebookHand over the rulebook or the Host Guide. Let them set up alone. Every question they ask shows a place where the rules or the components are unclear. Write it down and answer only after you have noted it.
  3. Play a full gameDo not interrupt. Do not give hints. Watch faces, not cards.
  4. Stay quiet and take notesUse the observation list below.
  5. Debrief (5 to 10 minutes)Ask the questions in the next section. Let people finish their sentences.
  6. Thank them and note your next changeWrite the three most important findings straight away, while you remember.

What to observe

Debrief questions

  1. In one sentence, how would you explain this game to a friend?
  2. Which card was your favourite? Why?
  3. Which card was the most confusing?
  4. Did any card feel unfair? Which?
  5. Were any facts hard to believe? (Ask them to point to the card.)
  6. Which of the three modes did you enjoy most? Least?
  7. Was the game too long, too short or about right?
  8. Was anything in the rules unclear?
  9. Would you play again? What would make you say yes?
  10. Who would you give this game to as a gift?
  11. What would you change first?

From findings to changes

FindingAction
Everyone guessed the same card rightMake the bluff more convincing, or replace the card. Check for giveaway words.
Nobody guessed a card rightCheck the fact. If it is true and verified, keep it as a surprise. Too many of these make the game feel unfair.
Players asked the same rules questionImprove how the Host Guide or rulebook explains it. Do not change the base rules.
Text was hard to readShorten the text, or enlarge it, then regenerate the card (Part 7).
Game too longPlay fewer cards, or play the short game for that group.
One player dominatedCheck the points spread. Try the optional Variants card or powers in the next test.
Players loved the castMention the characters in your listing and your images.
Second test with powers

After the base game works, run a short second test with the optional rules switched on (cast powers, Sharp Eyes). Only add an option to your listing if players enjoyed it.

Part 12Plan your second and third deck

One finished deck is a product. A system you can repeat is a catalog. The second deck is much faster than the first, because you already know the road. This part shows how to build on what you have.

What you reuse

  • Your folder structure and naming system (Part 6).
  • Your fact-checking routine and your source list (Part 5).
  • Your QA lists and your printing settings (Parts 6 and 8).
  • Your listing template, your customer messages and your price sheet (Part 10).
  • Your world's style sheet and cast, when you stay in the same world. Approved images are the most valuable thing you own.

Deck 2: a faster path

  1. Review deck 1Read your playtest notes, defect log and tracker. Write three lessons.
  2. Choose the next topicUse the scoring sheet again. Prefer a topic with a link to deck 1: same world, same audience.
  3. Reuse the approved style and castIn the same world, attach the existing approved images from the start. This removes much of phases 1 and 2.
  4. Verify as you goUse the same routine. Your source list from deck 1 may already cover part of the topic area.
  5. Test earlierRun the text playtest before any art, every time.
  6. Publish and compareUse the same tracker so you can compare deck 2 with deck 1.

Deck 3 and beyond: keep it sustainable

  • Batch your work. Do all topic research in one session, all fact checks in another, all images in a third.
  • Keep a topic pipeline. Hold five scored topics in your Idea bank so you never start from zero.
  • Do not clone yourself. Each deck needs a distinct topic and a distinct title. Near-duplicates confuse buyers and invite problems.
  • Let the numbers guide you. Build more of what your tracker and your reviews say people like.
  • Protect your time. A calendar with one deck in progress at a time beats three half-done decks.

The shortest version of everything

Pick a good topic. Verify it. Test the text. Then spend your effort on the art. Print a proof. Playtest it. List it honestly. Learn from what happens. Repeat.
